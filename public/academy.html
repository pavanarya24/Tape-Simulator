01–05

Foundations

The vocabulary everything after this depends on: how a quote works, who is aggressive, and how to measure it.

01 Bid, ask, and the cost of impatience Beginner · 4 min Foundation

Every quote has two prices: the bid, the highest price a buyer will pay, and the ask, the lowest price a seller will accept. The gap is the spread.

Buying at the ask fills you now and costs you the spread. Resting your own limit inside the spread gets a better price but may never fill. That trade-off sits underneath almost every scalping decision you will make, and Lesson 11 explains why the spread exists at all.

02 Tape colour: finding the aggressor Beginner · 3 min Foundation

Time and sales prints every trade as it happens. A green print means the buyer was impatient enough to lift the ask. A red print means the seller was impatient enough to hit the bid. Colour tells you who paid up — which is a different question from which way price moved.

One print means nothing. A run of prints on one side, especially with size, tells you who is in control right now. Beginners watch the last price; tape readers watch which side keeps paying the spread.

03 Size and imbalance on the ladder Beginner · 4 min Foundation

Level 2 shows resting size at each price. Comparing the two sides shows imbalance — which side has more firepower parked and waiting.

Heavy bid, thin ask — light resistance overhead

01 — Be precise about what you actually know

What imbalance tells you

Right now, one side has more displayed size resting and waiting than the other. That is a fact about the current book, nothing more.

What it does not tell you

Whether that size will still be there in five seconds, whether it is real or displayed to influence you, or which way price is about to move. Imbalance describes the book, not the future.

Common mistake

"The bid is three times the ask, so it has to go up." Displayed size is the cheapest thing in the market to fake — Lesson 6 covers exactly how — and even genuine size can be pulled the instant it is tested. Imbalance is one input to combine with the tape, never a signal on its own.

Key takeaways
  • Imbalance compares displayed resting size on the bid and the ask — a snapshot, not a forecast.
  • It raises the odds of a move, it does not confirm one.
  • Hidden size never appears in this comparison at all, so a "balanced" book can be lying to you too.
  • Always pair it with what the tape is actually doing (Lesson 2) before treating it as a reason to act.
04 How a real breakout triggers Beginner · 4 min Foundation

A genuine breakout is the thin side of the book being eaten. Ask size depletes print by print until there is nothing left, and price has nowhere to go but up. Downside is the mirror.

The trigger is the depletion, not the candle that closes above the level afterwards. By the time the breakout is obvious on a chart, the cheap entry and the tight stop have both gone.

05 Absorption: someone big is quietly taking it Intermediate · 5 min Foundation

Sometimes a wall is hit again and again but keeps refilling instead of depleting. That is absorption: a large participant taking the other side of aggressive flow without letting price move. It is the single most useful tell on the ladder.

Absorption at resistance often precedes a reversal once the big seller finishes, or a sharp breakout once they are done and step away. The tradeable moment is usually when the refill finally fails to appear — not while it is still refilling.

01 — The most important distinction in this lesson

What absorption tells you

A large participant is present at this exact price and is currently winning the exchange: aggressive orders are hitting them and going nowhere. Something real is happening right now.

What it does not tell you

Whether they will still be defending in ninety seconds, how much size they have left, or why they are there. Absorbers stop absorbing constantly, and when they do, price often moves fast in the direction they were just fighting.

Common mistake

"There's absorption at the low — buy it." Absorption is evidence a level is being defended, not proof it will hold. It is one input that needs confirmation: does price actually move away once the aggression stops, or does the level eventually give way anyway.

Key takeaways
  • Absorption is heavy aggression meeting heavier passive size, producing little or no price movement.
  • It is not a reversal signal — it is a stalemate that has not resolved yet.
  • The trade, if one exists, comes from what happens after the refill fails, not from the absorption itself.
  • It is distinct from exhaustion (aggression simply running out, Lesson 10) and from a trapped move (what happens to the losing side afterwards).
06–08

Reading participation

Vocabulary alone does not tell you whether what you are seeing is genuine. This block is about telling real participation from noise and traps, then filtering it by context.

06 Telling a real wall from a spoof Intermediate · 5 min Foundation

Spoofing is placing large orders with no intent to execute, to shape what everyone else believes. On a still frame a real wall and a fake one look identical. The difference is only visible over time.

Never trade displayed size alone. Wait to see whether it gets tested and trades, or vanishes the moment price arrives. The same discipline applies to every pattern in this course: the shape proposes, the tape confirms.

07 Context filters Intermediate · 4 min Foundation

The same tape pattern means different things in different conditions. Filter every setup:

  • Time of day — roughly 9:35 to 11:00 ET carries the most directional follow-through; midday is where good patterns go to die.
  • VWAP — a crude but effective bias filter. Longs above, shorts below, all else equal.
  • Spread and liquidity — wide, jumpy spreads mean your stop is less reliable than it looks on the chart.
  • News — fresh headlines produce flow that has nothing to do with the structure you were reading. Let it digest.
  • Relative volume — a stock trading at its normal volume is not in play, and patterns on it resolve at random.
A textbook absorption at 1:45pm on a stock with no catalyst and a five-cent spread is a different trade from the identical pattern at 9:45am on the day's most active name — even though your eyes see the same picture.
08 Risk and account rules Intermediate · 5 min Foundation

Rules used throughout this site, for a $25,000 account with roughly 4x intraday buying power and no PDT restriction:

  • Risk $25 to $50 per trade. No exceptions, regardless of how good it looks.
  • Stop for the day at three losing R. The trading desk in this site enforces this.
  • Around four trades a day. Scalping rewards selection, not activity.
  • Progression: replay historical data bar by bar, then simulator, then 100 shares live, then size up only once your process scores are consistent.
Buying power is headroom, not a target. Deploying it because a setup "feels certain" is the most reliable way to end a trading account.
Position size = risk ÷ stop distance. Risking $25 with a 12¢ stop is about 208 shares. If that maths feels like a constraint, that is the point of it.
09–11

Context and structure

Order flow without location is noise. Auction theory, Wyckoff structure and microstructure give you the map — where business got done, and why the market defends some prices and not others.

09 Auction theory: value area, point of control, initiative and response Advanced · 8 min Advanced

Everything so far describes who is trading. Auction theory describes where they are willing to trade, which is what tells you whether a break should be chased or faded.

The market as a two-sided auction

Price rises until buyers stop showing up, then falls until sellers dry up. The price where the most volume changed hands is the market's own statement about what is fair, for that session, by the only measure that counts: where business actually got done.

Point of control and value area

  • Point of control — the single price with the most volume. The centre of gravity.
  • Value area — the band holding roughly 70% of the session's volume, grown outward from the point of control.

Initiative versus responsive activity

  • Initiative — price pushes away from value and keeps going. Someone needs to be filled badly enough to pay ever-worse prices.
  • Responsive — price pokes outside value and gets dragged back in. The market disagreed that the new price was fair.
Trend day read. Price opens and stays outside yesterday's value with initiative aggression on the tape. Favour continuation, give trades room, stop fading.
Rotation day read. Price keeps poking out of value and snapping back. Fade the extremes toward the point of control and stop chasing breaks.

Your first half hour has one job: decide which of those two days you are in. Everything downstream — entry style, stop width, target — follows from that answer.

10 Effort versus result: springs, upthrusts and the Wyckoff frame Advanced · 9 min Advanced

Classical tape reading reduces to one habit: compare the effort spent with the result achieved. Volume is effort. Price change is result. When they disagree, someone is doing something.

The two readings

  • High effort, low result — heavy volume, price stuck. Absorption. Lesson 5's ladder pattern, viewed through volume instead.
  • Low effort, high result — thin volume, price travels. Nothing is in the way, and the move can continue cheaply.

The spring

A break below support that fails quickly and reverses. Shorts are filled at the worst price, then squeezed. Modern vocabularies call it a stop hunt or a liquidity sweep.

01 — Be precise about what a sweep actually confirms

What a spring or upthrust tells you

Resting orders beyond the obvious level got filled, and price failed to hold the new extreme — the side that broke out is now trapped and has to trade back the other way.

What it does not tell you

How far the reversal runs, or whether this is the only sweep of the day. Levels get swept more than once, and a spring that reclaims can still roll over later if nothing shows up to confirm the reversal on the way.

Common mistake

Buying the instant a low is broken because "it's probably a spring." A break only becomes a spring once it fails and reclaims on real opposite-side aggression — before that it is indistinguishable from a genuine breakdown. The table above is how you tell the two apart, and it needs the reclaim to have actually happened first.

Key takeaways
  • A spring or upthrust is a failed break, confirmed only after the fact by a fast reversal on climactic volume.
  • It tells you who is trapped, not how far the move back will go.
  • Scenario E in the Tape simulator plays the same level being swept in both directions in one session — the sweep is the setup, never the signal to join it.
11 Why liquidity gets run: dealers, adverse selection, and the plumbing under stop hunts Advanced · 8 min Advanced

This lesson explains why markets behave the way the previous two lessons describe. None of it requires a conspiracy; it falls out of incentives.

Who is on the other side

  • Dealers and market makers — quote both sides, earn the spread, manage inventory risk rather than predict direction.
  • Informed traders — have a genuine short-term edge and want to be filled before it decays.
  • Liquidity traders — trade for reasons unrelated to short-term value: hedging, rebalancing, ordinary retail flow.

Adverse selection, and why spreads exist

A dealer cannot tell in the moment whether the next order is uninformed, which is profit, or informed, which is a loss about to happen. The spread is the fee charged for that uncertainty. When spreads widen, dealers are telling you they think informed flow is around.

Why obvious levels get swept

Everyone is looking at the same chart, so stops and limits pile up just beyond the same swing highs and lows. That concentration is the cheapest available liquidity for anyone who needs to move real size. A large buyer does not sweep the lows to hurt you; they sweep the lows because that is where the shares are.

Wyckoff described the pattern in 1910. Microstructure theory, seventy years later, explained the incentive that produces it. Same behaviour, two vocabularies.

Your order type is a trade-off, not a preference

That last cell is adverse selection aimed at you. Your resting bid fills reliably in exactly one situation: when someone informed is selling into it.

A wick through an obvious level, a fast snap back, and a burst of tape at the extreme is liquidity being sourced where theory says it should be. Treat it as a cue.
Your own stop at the round number sits in the same pile. A small deliberate offset reduces the odds you are the liquidity, though it never eliminates them.

12 Process over outcome Advanced · 7 min Advanced

Everything above is worthless without the discipline to execute it the same way on the twentieth trade as on the first. Professional desks solve this by grading the process, never the profit.

What counts as a good trade

A good trade is one you would take again given the same information. A loss taken at the planned stop, in the planned size, on a planned setup, is a good trade. A win taken in triple size with no plan is a bad trade that happened to pay — and it teaches you the wrong lesson at exactly the moment you are most receptive.

Grade yourself on five questions, every trade

  1. Did you know your levels before the trade existed?
  2. Did you know the stop and the target before you clicked?
  3. Did size come from the stop distance, or from how sure you felt?
  4. Did you exit where you said you would?
  5. Did you wait for your setup, or manufacture one?

14 Order flow imbalance and the price impact model Advanced · 10 min Quant

Everything in Lessons 1 through 11 was qualitative: size, aggression, absorption, described in words. Market microstructure researchers measure the same phenomena as numbers, and the relationship turns out to be almost embarrassingly simple.

Order flow imbalance (OFI)

Define OFI over a short window as the net change in displayed depth, weighted by which side it happened on:

OFI = Δbid_depth · 1[bid price ≥ prev] − Δask_depth · 1[ask price ≤ prev]

In plain terms: count new size arriving at or above the previous best bid as positive, new size arriving at or below the previous best ask as negative, and net the two. A print that lifts the offer and depletes it contributes differently from one that just adds to the bid — OFI is built to capture that from the book itself, before you even look at the tape.

The empirical result that matters

Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov (2014) regressed short-horizon price change against OFI across liquid futures and equities and found something close to a straight line:

Δprice ≈ λ · OFI

λ (lambda) is a single coefficient — price impact per unit of order flow imbalance — and it holds up well within a stock or contract over a session, though it shifts across names and across volatility regimes. This is Kyle's lambda (1985) rediscovered empirically: a microstructure model built to describe how a market maker prices the risk of trading against someone informed collapses, in practice, to a linear relationship you can estimate from an afternoon of data.

01 — Why this explains the whole first half of this course

What OFI and lambda tell you

Absorption (Lesson 5) is what it looks like when λ is temporarily near zero at a price — huge OFI, no price response, because someone is absorbing the impact instead of letting the market clear at a new level. A breakout (Lesson 4) is λ snapping back to its normal size once that absorber steps away.

What it does not tell you

λ is estimated from the past and drifts. It is not a constant you can hardcode, and it spikes around news (Lesson 15's advanced-context lesson) in ways a session-average completely misses.

Common mistake

Treating imbalance from Lesson 3 and OFI here as the same thing. Imbalance is a snapshot of resting size. OFI is a flow measure — the change in that size, signed by direction — and it is the change, not the level, that the regression actually explains.

Key takeaways
  • OFI nets signed changes in depth at the best bid and ask over a short window.
  • Price change is close to linear in OFI, with a coefficient (λ) specific to the name and the moment.
  • Absorption is what a temporarily suppressed λ looks like on a ladder; a clean breakout is λ reasserting itself.
  • λ is not fixed — it is a rolling estimate, and it moves around news and volatility regime changes.
15 The statistics of your own edge Advanced · 11 min Quant

You cannot tell whether you have an edge by looking at your win rate. You need to know how much that win rate could plausibly be wrong by — and with the sample sizes most traders actually have, the honest answer is usually "a great deal."

Expectancy, restated properly

Per-trade expectancy in R is the mean of your realised R-multiples:

E[R] = (1/n) · Σ Rᵢ

That number alone tells you almost nothing about whether it is real. You also need its variance:

SE = σ(R) / √n

where σ(R) is the sample standard deviation of your R-multiples and n is the trade count. A 95% confidence interval on your true expectancy is approximately E[R] ± 1.96 · SE. If that interval contains zero, your data cannot yet distinguish your process from a coin flip — regardless of how good the story behind each trade felt.

Why n = 20 tells you almost nothing

R-multiples on a discretionary strategy are noisy — a typical σ(R) sits around 1.0 to 1.5. At n = 20 with σ(R) = 1.2, your standard error is about 0.27R, so a 95% interval is roughly ±0.53R around whatever mean you measured. An observed edge of +0.4R per trade — which would be an excellent result — is statistically indistinguishable from zero at that sample size. You need somewhere in the range of n = 80 to 150 trades, depending on your actual variance, before a modest real edge reliably clears its own confidence interval.

Same measured edge, same formula — only the sample size changed, and it's the difference between "unknown" and "real"

01 — What this means for a discretionary reader, specifically

What a wide CI tells you

Your sample is too small to know your edge yet. That is not a verdict — it is the honest state of the evidence. Keep trading the process, keep logging, and let the interval narrow.

What it does not tell you

It does not mean you have no edge, and a positive average with a wide interval does not mean you do. Both are simply unresolved until n grows or you get unlucky enough to see the interval cross zero decisively.

Common mistake

Doubling size after ten winning trades. Ten trades is not evidence of anything — it is within the range a strategy with zero true edge produces by chance alarmingly often. The Report Card computes your actual confidence interval from your logged desk trades so you are not guessing.

Key takeaways
  • Expectancy without a confidence interval is a number with no error bar — treat it as provisional.
  • Standard error shrinks with √n, not n — quadrupling your sample only halves your uncertainty.
  • Realistic sample sizes for a discretionary reader to trust a modest edge run into the hundreds of trades, not tens.
  • A confidence interval that contains zero is not failure — it is an honest "not enough data yet."
16 Sizing with Kelly, and why nobody trades full Kelly Advanced · 10 min Quant

Lesson 8 fixed your risk at a flat $25 to $50 per trade regardless of conviction — a deliberately simple rule for a reason this lesson makes precise. Once you have an actual edge, there is a formula for the size that grows an account fastest. It is almost never the size a sane person should use.

The Kelly criterion

For a strategy with win probability W and a win/loss size ratio R (average win size divided by average loss size), the growth-optimal fraction of capital to risk per bet is:

f* = W − (1 − W) / R

Example: a 45% win rate with an average win of 2R against an average loss of 1R gives f* = 0.45 − 0.55/2 = 0.175 — risk 17.5% of account equity per trade at full Kelly. Reading that number should make you uncomfortable, which is the point of the next section.

Why full Kelly is not investable

  • W and R are estimates, not truths. Lesson 15 just showed you how wide your confidence interval is at realistic sample sizes. Kelly's formula assumes you know W and R exactly; feed it an optimistic estimate and it happily recommends a fraction that ruins you.
  • Full Kelly has brutal variance. Even with the true parameters, full Kelly portfolios commonly see 50%+ drawdowns on the way to their long-run growth rate. Nobody survives that psychologically, and most accounts have a real-world stop-out point Kelly's math does not know about.
  • Returns are not identically distributed across regimes. Kelly assumes a stable repeated game. Lesson 7's time-of-day and Lesson 15's advanced-context material both say your actual W and R shift by session and by news proximity — the inputs to the formula are moving targets.

Fractional Kelly, the version people actually use

Most quantitative shops that size by Kelly at all use a quarter- to half-Kelly fraction:

f_used = k · f*,   k ∈ [0.25, 0.5]

Quarter-Kelly cuts long-run growth rate by roughly half relative to full Kelly, but cuts variance by close to three-quarters — a trade almost everyone should take. It is also a built-in hedge against the estimation error in the previous section: if your true edge is actually zero and your measured W and R were noise, quarter-Kelly loses you a quarter as fast as full Kelly would.

01 — Reconciling this with Lesson 8's flat $25

What Kelly tells you

Once your Report Card shows a statistically credible edge (Lesson 15's confidence interval clears zero with a reasonable sample), Kelly gives you a principled ceiling for how aggressively you could size — and fractional Kelly a principled, survivable version of it.

What it does not tell you

Anything useful before you have that sample. Feeding Kelly an edge estimated from 15 trades is feeding it noise, and it will size you into ruin exactly as confidently as it would size you into real growth. Flat risk isn't a lesser method — it's the correct one until Kelly has real inputs to work with.

Common mistake

Computing f* from a hot streak and sizing up immediately. Kelly is only as good as its inputs, and a hot streak is the single least reliable moment to estimate W and R — recency bias and small samples point the same direction at once.

Key takeaways
  • f* = W − (1−W)/R gives the growth-optimal risk fraction for a known edge.
  • Full Kelly is rarely used in practice: estimation error and drawdown depth make it unsurvivable.
  • Quarter to half Kelly is the industry-standard compromise between growth and survivability.
  • None of this is usable until your sample size (Lesson 15) actually supports the W and R you're plugging in.

Two more layers of vocabulary used on professional order-flow platforms: footprint charts and cumulative delta as a plotted, tradeable indicator, and the order types and participants that actually populate the tape. The Charts tab now plots CVD live under every generated chart.

17 Footprint charts, delta, and the four kinds of divergence Advanced · 11 min Quant

Everything so far reads volume as a single bar-height number. A footprint chart splits that number by side and by exact price tick, turning one candle into a small grid: buy volume and sell volume, printed against every price the bar traded through.

The mechanical lifecycle of a single trade, one tool at a time

Delta and cumulative delta

Delta is the simplest possible order-flow number:

Delta = Ask-side volume − Bid-side volume

Ask-side volume is trades that lifted the offer (buyers paying up); bid-side volume is trades that hit the bid (sellers giving it away). A single bar's delta tells you which side was more willing to pay the spread during that bar. Cumulative Volume Delta (CVD) is the running total of every bar's delta across the session — a scoreboard of aggression that never resets, plotted directly beneath the candles in the Charts tab.

The four named divergences, and where you've already seen every one

  • Classic bearish divergence — price makes a higher high, CVD makes a lower high or stalls. This is your Upthrust (Lesson 10) expressed in delta terms.
  • Classic bullish divergence — price makes a lower low, CVD holds or makes a higher low. This is your Spring (Lesson 10) expressed in delta terms.
  • Hidden divergence (continuation) — in an established trend, price makes a shallow higher low but CVD makes a lower low: sellers threw volume at the market and still couldn't break it. This is the pullback-and-hold read behind Initiative trading (Lesson 9), one step more specific.
  • Absorption-based divergence — CVD surges hard in one direction while price does not move at all. This is Absorption (Lesson 5), named exactly the same way in both vocabularies.
A rigorous version of the "friction matrix": four quadrants from price × CVD agreement, plus absorption sitting outside the grid entirely because price isn't trending yet
Every generated chart in the Charts tab now has a genuine, computed CVD line beneath it, engineered to show the correct divergence signature for whichever pattern it contains. Read the CVD panel before you reveal — the chart's own cvdRead tells you which of these four it was.

01 — CVD and the OFI from Lesson 14 are not the same measurement

What CVD tells you

Trade-based aggression: literally, which side paid the spread, summed across the session. It only uses executions — the Time & Sales tape — and needs no order-book access at all.

What OFI (Lesson 14) measures instead

Depth-based imbalance: change in resting size at the best bid and ask, whether or not a trade even happened. Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov's regression used OFI, not CVD, and the two correlate strongly but are computed from different data and can disagree — a large iceberg absorbing flow moves CVD hard while barely moving OFI, since the displayed depth never actually changes.

Common mistake

Treating a CVD divergence as a standalone entry signal. It is a context tool — Lesson 7's location-first framework still applies. A textbook absorption-based divergence at a random midday price with no structural significance is a much weaker read than the identical CVD shape at a level from Lesson 9's value area or point of control.

Key takeaways
  • Delta = ask-side volume minus bid-side volume, per bar; CVD is its running sum.
  • Four named divergence types cover almost everything you've already learned to spot visually.
  • CVD (trade-based) and OFI (depth-based, Lesson 14) measure related but distinct things.
  • A divergence is context for a location-based decision, never a signal by itself.
18 Order types, who's actually on the tape, and where this works at all Advanced · 10 min Quant

Two remaining gaps: the specific order types that produce the patterns you already read, and an honest answer to which markets this entire course actually applies to.

Iceberg orders — the mechanism behind absorption

An iceberg (reserve) order shows only a small slice of a much larger order on the book. When that slice trades, it silently refreshes to the same displayed size, again and again, while the tape prints thousands of shares against one price. This is the literal mechanism behind absorption (Lesson 5) — absorption is what an iceberg looks like from outside; the iceberg is the order type that produces it.

Intermarket sweep orders — urgency made visible

An Intermarket Sweep Order (ISO) routes simultaneously across multiple exchanges to take resting liquidity at several price levels at once, bypassing the usual order-protection checks. On the tape it looks like a millisecond burst tearing through two or three price levels in one motion — a strong tell that a large participant needs to be filled now, not eventually.

Stacked imbalances — the footprint-level version of Lesson 3

Lesson 3 compared resting size on the book. A stacked (or diagonal) imbalance is the executed version at the tick level: three or more consecutive price ticks where one side's traded volume overwhelms the other by roughly 3:1 or more. A run of these is often read as short-term structural support or resistance, since it marks exactly where one side was recently overwhelming the other.

01 — What years of screen time actually buys you

What experience earns you

Calibration — a felt sense for what a genuine iceberg, a real sweep, or a stacked imbalance looks like on your specific instrument, at your specific time of day. This course gets you the vocabulary; only repetition gets you the calibration.

What it does not earn you

Immunity from the ~30% of clean-looking setups that fail anyway (the Drill and Desk have been showing you this the whole time), or a shortcut around Lesson 15's sample-size math. Experience improves your read; it does not change the statistics.

Common mistake

Reading a fragmented equity's tape as if it were a centralized futures book. Dark-pool and off-exchange prints report late and out of sequence on many feeds — a "sweep" on a fragmented equity tape carries far less certainty than the identical shape on a single-book future.

Key takeaways
  • An iceberg order is the mechanism; absorption is what it looks like from outside.
  • An ISO sweeps several price levels across venues at once — a tell for genuine urgency.
  • A stacked imbalance is Lesson 3's book-depth imbalance, measured instead in executed volume at the tick level.
  • Data quality is not uniform: futures are cleanest, equities are fragmented, forex has no single tape, and crypto is venue-specific.
